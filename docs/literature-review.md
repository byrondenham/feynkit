# GKZ Systems and Feynman Integrals: A Literature Review

## 1. Introduction

The analytic evaluation of Feynman integrals is a central problem in perturbative quantum field theory, intimately connected to cutting-edge questions in modern mathematics. Among the most striking recent threads in this connection is the recognition that almost all Feynman integrals can be understood as *A-hypergeometric functions* in the sense of Gelfand, Kapranov, and Zelevinsky (GKZ). This perspective unifies, on a single footing, three seemingly different facets of the theory: holonomic systems of partial differential equations, multivariate hypergeometric series, and Euler-Mellin-type integrals. It also embeds the entire calculus of Feynman integrals into a polyhedral framework in which the kinematic content of a graph is encoded as a convex lattice polytope, the Newton polytope of a graph polynomial, whose triangulations, volume, automorphisms, and singular hypersurfaces translate directly into series representations, master-integral counts, transformation identities, and Landau singularities.

The conjecture that Feynman integrals constitute a kind of generalised hypergeometric function goes back to Regge and was made precise in the work of Kashiwara and Kawai, who showed that Feynman integrals satisfy holonomic differential systems whose singularities are governed by Landau loci. The decisive modern development, however, was the recognition, formalised independently by de la Cruz (2019) and Klausen (2020), and built on the parametric representation of Lee and Pomeransky, that the differential system is in fact (isomorphic to) a GKZ system, constructed from a single graph polynomial whose monomial exponents form an integer matrix $A$. The present review surveys this development through the supplied source papers, locating each contribution within a coherent picture: the GKZ formalism (Sec. 2), its application to Feynman integrals via the Lee-Pomeransky representation (Sec. 3), the explicit solution of the resulting systems by triangulations and canonical series (Sec. 4), the symmetry and transformation theory of the underlying polytopes (Sec. 5), the algorithmic machinery for classifying lattice polytopes (Sec. 6), the broader context of polytopal physics in conformal field theory and cosmology (Sec. 7), and finally an assessment of computational aspects, limitations, and open directions (Sec. 8).

## 2. Mathematical Foundations: GKZ Systems

The theory of A-hypergeometric (or GKZ) functions was developed in the late 1980s by Gelfand, Graev, Kapranov, Zelevinsky and collaborators as a comprehensive framework for hypergeometric functions in several variables. As Klausen (2020) and de la Cruz (2019) emphasise, the framework reconciles three classical characterisations of the Gauss function ${}_2F_1$, as a power series, as an Euler-type integral, and as a solution of a holonomic differential equation, into a single object naturally indexed by a finite point configuration.

### 2.1 Definition

A GKZ system is specified by an integer matrix $A \in \mathbb{Z}^{(n+1)\times N}$ of full rank $n+1$ whose columns span the integer lattice and lie in a common affine hyperplane, and a parameter vector $\beta \in \mathbb{C}^{n+1}$. Without loss of generality $A$ is taken with a row of ones, so its columns may be regarded as lattice points in $\mathbb{Z}^n$. The GKZ ideal $H_A(\beta)$ in the Weyl algebra is generated by

- the **toric operators** $\partial^u - \partial^v$ for all $u, v \in \mathbb{N}^N$ with $A u = A v$, and
- the **Euler / homogeneity operators** $\sum_j a_{ij} z_j \partial_j + \beta_i$, $i = 0, \dots, n$.

A holomorphic function annihilated by $H_A(\beta)$ is called *A-hypergeometric*. Weinzierl's textbook (2022) presents this construction in its standard form, including the associated lattice $L = \ker_\mathbb{Z} A$ of relations and the Laurent polynomial $p(z, x) = \sum_j x_j z^{a_j}$ encoded by the columns of $A$.

### 2.2 The polytope and the holonomic rank

A central structural result, stated as Theorem 2.2 of Klausen (2020) following Gelfand-Kapranov-Zelevinsky and Saito-Sturmfels-Takayama, is that for a full-dimensional configuration and very generic $\beta$,

$$
\operatorname{rank} H_A(\beta) \;=\; \operatorname{vol}_0(\operatorname{Conv}(A)),
$$

where $\operatorname{vol}_0$ is the lattice-normalised volume of the convex hull of the columns of $A$. The rank gives the dimension of the local solution space; the regular triangulations of the Newton polytope $\operatorname{Conv}(A)$ provide an explicit construction of bases of solutions in terms of $\Gamma$-series. De la Cruz (2019) gives an inequality form, $\operatorname{rank} H_A(\kappa) \geq \operatorname{vol}(A)$, with equality for generic parameters and possible "rank jumps" for non-generic $\kappa$, a phenomenon that, as we shall see, is intimately related to relations between Feynman integrals.

### 2.3 Series and integral solutions

Two complementary constructions of solutions are central to the GKZ framework:

- **$\Gamma$-series.** For $\xi \in \mathbb{C}^N$ with $A\xi + \beta = 0$, the formal series $\varphi_\xi(z) = \sum_{l \in L} z^{l+\xi} / \Gamma(\xi + l + 1)$ is annihilated by $H_A(\beta)$. Choosing an index set $\sigma$ such that $A_\sigma$ is invertible converts this into a power series $\phi_\sigma$ whose convergence and non-degeneracy require $\beta$ to be *very generic* with respect to $\sigma$. Klausen (2020) and de la Cruz (2019) both show that for a regular unimodular triangulation $T$ of the Newton polytope, the family $\{\phi_\sigma\}_{\sigma \in T}$ forms a basis of the solution space with a common region of convergence (Klausen's Theorem 2.3, citing Gelfand-Kapranov-Zelevinsky).
- **Euler-type integrals.** Solutions can equivalently be presented as integrals
  $$ I_b(\kappa) = \int_\Omega z^\alpha\, b(c, z)^\beta\, \frac{dz_1}{z_1}\wedge \cdots \wedge \frac{dz_N}{z_N}, $$
  over an appropriate cycle $\Omega$. While GKZ originally worked with compact cycles, the work of Berkesch, Forsgård, and Passare (BFP), as reviewed in detail by de la Cruz (2019), provided explicit *non-compact* cycles constructed from connected components of the complement of the *coamoeba* of the variety $\{b = 0\}$, the image of the variety under the argument mapping. These BFP cycles are the appropriate ones for representing Feynman integrals, whose natural integration domain is the positive orthant $\mathbb{R}_+^N$.

### 2.4 Canonical series via Gröbner deformation

To compute series solutions algorithmically, Saito, Sturmfels, and Takayama (SST) developed a generalisation of the Frobenius method using Gröbner deformations. De la Cruz (2019) presents this *canonical series algorithm* in detail. The procedure takes a generic weight vector $w$, computes the toric ideal $I_A$, its initial ideal $\operatorname{in}_w(I_A)$, the *standard pairs* of $\operatorname{in}_w(I_A)$, and from these the *fake indicial ideal*
$$
\operatorname{fin}_w(H_A(\kappa)) \;=\; \operatorname{ind}_w(I_A) + \langle A\theta - \kappa^T \rangle,
$$
whose roots, the *fake exponents*, are the leading terms of the canonical series. The Macaulay2 D-modules package implements this directly, and an explicit example for the so-called "party hat" Feynman integral is worked out in the appendix of de la Cruz (2019). Weinzierl (2022) gives a textbook treatment of the same algorithm using logarithmic series and the indicial ideal.

## 3. Feynman Integrals in the Lee-Pomeransky Representation

The bridge from GKZ theory to Feynman integrals runs through the parametric representation of Lee and Pomeransky.

### 3.1 The Lee-Pomeransky polynomial

A scalar $L$-loop Feynman integral with $n$ propagators in $d$ space-time dimensions admits the parametric form, derived by Lee and Pomeransky and reviewed in Klausen (2020), de la Cruz (2019), and Weinzierl (2022),
$$
I_\Gamma(\nu, d, p, m) \;=\; \frac{\Gamma(d/2)}{\Gamma(d/2 - \omega)\,\Gamma(\nu)} \int_{\mathbb{R}_+^n} dx\, x^{\nu - 1}\, G(x)^{-d/2},
$$
where $G = U + F$ is the sum of the first and second Symanzik polynomials. The crucial observation is that *all* the analytic structure is now packaged into a single polynomial $G$. Writing
$$
G(x) \;=\; \sum_{a_j \in A} z_j\, x^{a_j},
$$
the matrix $A$ of monomial exponents (augmented with a row of ones) is precisely the data of a GKZ system. The kinematic invariants and squared masses become the coefficients $z_j$.

### 3.2 The generalised Feynman integral

To bring the Feynman integral into the GKZ framework one follows de la Cruz (2019) and Klausen (2020) in promoting the coefficients of $G$ to *indeterminate* variables. The resulting *generalised Feynman integral*, sometimes called a toric or Euler-Mellin Feynman integral, is then a particular A-hypergeometric function in the variables $z = (z_1, \dots, z_N)$, where $N$ is the number of monomials in $G$. The physical Feynman integral is recovered as a specific *fibre*: a particular set of values of $z$ determined by the kinematics. The polytope $\Delta_G = \operatorname{Conv}(A)$ is sometimes called the *Feynman polytope*.

### 3.3 The proof that Feynman integrals are A-hypergeometric

The key theorem, that the generalised Feynman integral satisfies the GKZ system associated with the Lee-Pomeransky polynomial, was proven independently by de la Cruz (2019, Theorem in Sec. 3) and Klausen (2020, Theorem 3.1). The proofs are short and elegant: the toric relations $\partial^u - \partial^v$ for $A u = A v$ follow from the homogeneity of the integrand under simultaneous shifts of the $z_j$, while the Euler relations follow from a scaling argument $J_A(\nu, s^{a_{b_1}} z_1, \dots, s^{a_{b_N}} z_N) = s^{-\nu_b} J_A(\nu, z)$ combined with differentiation in $s$. As Klausen notes, this confirms, and makes constructive, the earlier result of Nasrollahpoursamami (2016) that the Feynman integral satisfies a system isomorphic to a GKZ system.

A particular consequence is that *every coefficient in the Laurent expansion in dimensional regularisation* is itself a Horn hypergeometric function. Klausen (2020) shows this by noting that derivatives with respect to parameters of Horn hypergeometric series are again Horn hypergeometric series (citing Sadykov), so the $\epsilon$-expansion of the GKZ-derived series representation produces, term by term, hypergeometric functions of the same combinatorial type but of higher degree.

### 3.4 Convergence, meromorphic continuation, and the relative interior

The polytope perspective also illuminates convergence and analytic continuation. Klausen (2020) collates several results:

- **Convergence (Theorem 1.4).** The Feynman integral converges absolutely in the Euclidean region whenever the rescaled propagator powers $\operatorname{Re}(\nu) / \operatorname{Re}(\nu_0)$ lie in the *relative interior* of the Newton polytope $\Delta_G$. If the polytope is not full-dimensional, the integral is neither dimensionally nor analytically regularisable, a polyhedral characterisation of the well-known difficulty with massless tadpoles.
- **Meromorphic continuation (Theorem 1.5).** The integral admits a meromorphic continuation of the form $J_A(\nu, z) = \Phi_A(\nu, z) \prod_{j=1}^k \Gamma(b_j \operatorname{Re}\nu_0 - m_j^T \operatorname{Re}\nu)$, where the product runs over the facets of the Newton polytope and $\Phi_A$ is entire in $\nu$. The poles, and hence the divergences in dimensional regularisation, are encoded directly in the facet inequalities of $\Delta_G$.

These statements are direct adaptations of results of Berkesch-Forsgård-Passare for general Euler-Mellin integrals; the Feynman integral is thus a special case of an Euler-Mellin integral defined by a single polynomial.

### 3.5 Other parametric representations

The GKZ approach is not specific to the Lee-Pomeransky polynomial. As Klausen (2020) remarks, the same machinery applies to the original Feynman parametric representation when either $U$ or $F$ alone appears. The case where $F$ drops out corresponds to the *period* integrals studied in the QFT-motives literature and is non-generic from a GKZ perspective; the case where $U$ drops out arises for "marginal" graphs with $\omega = d/2$ (e.g. the banana integrals at $\nu_i = 1$, $d = 2$) and is, by contrast, easier because no auxiliary variables are needed in $U$. The Baikov representation is also amenable in principle, and recent literature has explored this avenue.

## 4. Solving GKZ Systems for Feynman Integrals

### 4.1 Series representations from triangulations

Once a Feynman integral is identified as an A-hypergeometric function, a regular unimodular triangulation $T$ of $\Delta_G$ provides an explicit basis of $\Gamma$-series, with one series per top-dimensional simplex $\sigma \in T$. Klausen (2020, Theorem 3.2) gives a closed-form expression:
$$
J_A(\nu, z) \;=\; \sum_{\sigma \in T} z_\sigma^{-A_\sigma^{-1} \nu} \sum_{\lambda \in \mathbb{N}_0^{|\bar\sigma|}} \frac{\Gamma(A_\sigma^{-1}\nu + A_\sigma^{-1} A_{\bar\sigma}\lambda)}{\lambda!}\, \frac{z_{\bar\sigma}^\lambda}{(-z_\sigma)^{A_\sigma^{-1} A_{\bar\sigma} \lambda}}.
$$
This formula expresses the Feynman integral as a sum over simplices of *Horn hypergeometric series*, with prefactors given by products of $\Gamma$-functions read off directly from the polytope geometry. The number of independent series equals the lattice volume of $\Delta_G$, in accordance with the GKZ rank theorem. Klausen tabulates this volume, together with the number of regular triangulations, regular unimodular triangulations, and master integrals, for many standard one-, two-, and three-loop topologies (bubble, sunset, vertex, dunce's cap, box, kite, banana).

De la Cruz (2019) takes a complementary, more algorithmic view, working through the SST canonical series algorithm in detail for several examples: the massless bubble (codimension-zero, requiring deformation), the massless triangle and $L$-loop "cantaloupe" (banana) graph, the one-mass bubble, the one-mass sunset, the single-scale "party hat", the on-shell massless box (which reproduces a ${}_3F_2$), and a codimension-two example. In each case the canonical series algorithm reproduces, and in some cases generalises to non-integer propagator powers, known results from the hypergeometric-functions and Mellin-Barnes literature.

### 4.2 Codimension issues and deformation

A practical obstacle is that certain Feynman polynomials lead to GKZ matrices of codimension zero (i.e., the lattice $L = \ker A$ is trivial), in which case the canonical series algorithm cannot generate non-trivial solutions. De la Cruz (2019) introduces a systematic *deformation*: one adds an extra monomial (or several) to $G$, computes the deformed integral as a sum of canonical series with the SST algorithm, and then takes the limit as the auxiliary coefficients vanish. The massless bubble is deformed to the one-mass bubble; the massless cantaloupe is deformed by a single auxiliary monomial chosen so that the resulting matrix has codimension one, leading to a Gauss hypergeometric ${}_2F_1$ representation that reproduces known results in the limit.

Klausen (2020) discusses a closely related issue: not every Feynman polytope admits a unimodular triangulation, even though many do. For polytopes that do not, Klausen gives two strategies: (i) restrict to a sub-triangulation that is unimodular; (ii) use a result of Knudsen that a sufficiently dilated polytope $k\Delta_G$ always admits a unimodular triangulation, and recover the original integral as a limit. He further conjectures, on the basis of explicit checks up to three loops, that *all off-shell Feynman graphs admit at least one unimodular triangulation*, an empirical observation about Feynman polytopes that is one of the reasons the GKZ approach is so widely applicable in practice.

### 4.3 Master integrals and the rank-volume relation

A particularly suggestive consequence of the polytope perspective concerns the count of master integrals. For *generic* coefficients $z$, the dimension of the solution space equals the lattice volume $\operatorname{vol}_0 \Delta_G$. At the *physical* values of $z$, Bitoun et al. (2019) show that the number of master integrals is the Euler characteristic $C = (-1)^n \chi((\mathbb{C}^*)^n \setminus \{G = 0\})$. In general $C \leq n!\,\operatorname{Vol}(\Delta_G)$, with $\operatorname{Vol}$ the Euclidean volume. This bound equals $\operatorname{vol}_0 \Delta_G$ when the exponent differences span $\mathbb{Z}^n$; in that case strict inequality signals that the limit from the generalised to the physical Feynman integral imposes linear relations among the $\Gamma$-series. Klausen (2020) makes the analogy explicit: the linear combination of $\Gamma$-series in (3.4) of his paper plays the role of a decomposition into a basis of master integrals, and different triangulations correspond to different master-integral bases related by transformation identities of hypergeometric functions.

### 4.4 The $\epsilon$-expansion

For dimensionally regularised integrals one needs the Laurent expansion in $\epsilon = (4 - d)/2$. Klausen (2020) shows that this reduces to differentiating the Horn hypergeometric series with respect to its parameters, using the identities $(a)_{m+n} = (a)_m (a+m)_n$ and the Sadykov reduction formulas, so that all coefficients of the $\epsilon$-expansion remain Horn hypergeometric functions of the same combinatorial type, of progressively higher degree. The full massive sunset is worked out in detail in Klausen's Section 4 as a non-trivial illustration: $G$ has $N=10$ monomials, the lattice volume is 10, and the integral is expressed as a sum of ten hypergeometric series whose $\epsilon$-expansion yields multivariate generalisations of polylogarithms.

## 5. Symmetries and Transformations

A central insight of the GKZ programme is that *transformation identities* between hypergeometric functions are reflections of *combinatorial symmetries* of the underlying polytope. The supplied papers contain three substantial contributions on this theme.

### 5.1 Transformations of A-hypergeometric functions (Forsgård, Matusevich, Sobieska, 2019)

Forsgård, Matusevich, and Sobieska (FMS) systematically study the transformation theory of A-hypergeometric functions using the BFP integral representations. They identify three sources of transformations:

1. **Changes of variables** in the integral that preserve the cycle up to homotopy. If $\varphi: \mathbb{R}_+^N \to \mathbb{R}_+^N$ is a diffeomorphism with non-vanishing toric Jacobian and $g = \varphi^* f$, then the corresponding A-hypergeometric integrals are related by an explicit formula involving $\varphi$.
2. **Polytope symmetries.** If $T A = A P$ for an invertible integer matrix $T$ and a permutation matrix $P$, then $A$ and $A P$ define the same point configuration up to a change of coordinates, and the corresponding A-hypergeometric functions are related by $F(\beta, x) = R(\beta) F(T\beta, x P)$ for some constant prefactor $R$. As printed, FMS's Corollary 4.1 has $x P$ on the right, but the substitution in its proof gives $x P^{-1}$, so the identity reads $F(\beta, x P) = |\det T|\, F(T\beta, x)$; the two forms agree only when $P$ is an involution, as in all of FMS's worked examples, and de la Cruz (2024, eq. 14) repeats the printed form. FMS prove a *partial converse* (Theorem 4.4): every linear transformation of an A-hypergeometric function valid for generic parameters arises from such a polytope symmetry.
3. **Monoid-ring automorphisms** of the underlying toric variety, which give a third source of transformations.

FMS connect these structural results to classical Pfaff and Euler transformations of ${}_2F_1$, the four families of Appell functions, and Lauricella's $F_C^{(m)}$, exhibiting in each case the polytope symmetry responsible for the identity.

### 5.2 Polytope symmetries of Feynman integrals (de la Cruz, 2024)

De la Cruz (2024) applies the FMS framework directly to Feynman integrals. The strategy is operational: given the matrix $A$ of monomial exponents of $G = U + F$, identify the symmetries of the Newton polytope $\operatorname{Conv}(A)$, that is, the pairs $(T, P)$ with $T A = A P$, and translate each such pair into a transformation identity of the (generalised) Feynman integral. For Feynman integrals, the prefactor $R(\kappa)$ in the FMS theorem is unity, and the cycle is invariant under the relevant change of variables up to homotopy.

De la Cruz reports concrete results:
- For the **one-loop massive bubble** ($N = 4$), a direct search yields eight pairs $(T, P)$, recovering the eightfold matrix group of the Gauss ${}_2F_1$.
- For **fully massive banana graphs** at $L$ loops, only the obvious relabelling symmetries persist; for partially massive cases ($w = 10\dots 0$, $w = 10\dots 01$), additional symmetries emerge. Direct searches were performed up to two loops and PALP was used up to five loops, with results summarised in a single table.
- For **massless $n$-gons** (one-loop $n$-point integrals) with $n$ up to 8, the count of symmetries is 72 for $n = 4$ and 2 for higher $n$.
- For **on-shell ladders** up to three loops, the symmetries are computed and applied to study finite on-shell ladder integrals.

The general algorithmic problem, given a permutation $P_{\text{try}}$, decide whether there exists $T$ with $T A P_{\text{try}} = A$, reduces to a linear system. For matrices with up to about 11 monomials a direct search is feasible; beyond that, PALP is used to compute the polytope's automorphism group (via the normal form of $A$) and the corresponding $T$ matrices are recovered by solving the linear system.

### 5.3 Connection to classical hypergeometric transformations

A simple but conceptually important observation, made explicit by both FMS and de la Cruz (2024), is that classical transformation formulas, the Pfaff transformation of ${}_2F_1$, for instance, are *consequences* of polytope symmetries. From the GKZ vantage point, the bewildering profusion of identities for multivariate hypergeometric functions is reorganised as the orbit structure of a single polytope under its symmetry group. Each such symmetry relates the integral at two parameter vectors, $\beta$ and $T\beta$, with the coefficients permuted. The IBP-like relations among Feynman integrals, the subject of the long-running programme of "differential reduction" of generalised hypergeometric functions (Bytev-Kalmykov and others), have a different source: the contiguity (shift) operators and the toric operators of the GKZ system, which relate integrals whose parameters differ by integer shifts.

## 6. Polytope Classification: Algorithmic Infrastructure

Because every step of the GKZ approach to Feynman integrals, convergence, master-integral count, series representations, transformation identities, symmetries, is controlled by the lattice polytope $\Delta_G$, the algorithmic theory of convex lattice polytopes becomes physically relevant. Two of the supplied papers treat this layer directly.

### 6.1 Normal forms (Grinis & Kasprzyk, 2013)

Grinis and Kasprzyk address the fundamental computational problem of deciding whether two convex lattice polytopes are isomorphic with respect to a lattice automorphism. They describe two complementary approaches:

1. **Dynamic isomorphism testing** via the labelled face graph $\mathscr{G}(P)$ of the polytope. Reducing the polytope-isomorphism problem to a graph-isomorphism problem permits the use of mature tools such as McKay's *Nauty*. This approach handles rational polytopes and polytopes of non-zero codimension, allows the computation of the affine automorphism group $\operatorname{AffAut}(P)$, and was implemented in Magma.
2. **Normal form computation** following Kreuzer and Skarke's PALP package, in which row and column permutations are applied to the vertex-facet pairing matrix to bring it into a maximal form $PM_{\max}$, defining a canonical ordering of vertices; the basis is fixed by the Hermite normal form. Grinis and Kasprzyk give an improved algorithm exploiting matrix automorphisms ("Symm") which substantially outperforms PALP on highly symmetric polytopes, a feature directly relevant to Feynman polytopes, whose symmetries grow factorially with the number of monomials.

The paper also gives detailed pseudo-code, an example using the 24-cell as the unique 4-dimensional reflexive polytope with $|{\operatorname{Aut}(P)}| = 1152$, and a section on normal forms of Laurent polynomials. For Feynman integrals, this is the toolkit that makes de la Cruz (2024)'s symmetry searches possible at higher loop orders.

### 6.2 The unimodular isomorphism problem (Liu & Cai, 2025)

Liu and Cai address the closely related *unimodular isomorphism problem* (UIP): given two convex lattice polytopes $P, P' \subset \mathbb{Z}^n$, decide whether there exists an affine map $U: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}^n$ with $U(\mathbb{Z}^n) = \mathbb{Z}^n$ and $U(P) = P'$. They show that:

1. **UIP is graph-isomorphism hard.** A polynomial-time reduction from the graph-isomorphism problem to UIP is constructed by associating a lattice polytope $P(G)$ to each graph $G$ via the convex hull of the origin, the standard basis vectors, and edge-associated vectors $e_i + e_j$.
2. **A statistical zero-knowledge proof system exists for UIP.** Building on Gaussian-sampling techniques (Gentry-Peikert-Vaikuntanathan; Brakerski et al.) and the recent work of Ducas and van Woerden on lattice isomorphism, the authors construct an average-case distribution over unimodular-isomorphism classes and a zero-knowledge proof system for UIP.
3. **An explicit algorithm computes all unimodular affine transformations** mapping one polytope to another by reducing the problem to label-preserving isomorphism of minimum spanning trees in a weighted vertex-edge graph.

While the Liu-Cai paper is purely mathematical and does not mention Feynman integrals, its results have direct practical implications for the GKZ programme: identifying when two Feynman polytopes (associated, e.g., with different graphs or different kinematic limits) are unimodularly isomorphic determines when their A-hypergeometric integrals coincide up to a coordinate change. This is the algorithmic backbone that supports, and bounds the complexity of, the symmetry searches of de la Cruz (2024) and the classification programme implicit in Klausen (2020)'s tables.

## 7. Connections and Applications

### 7.1 Conformal field theory: simplex integrals (Bzowski, McFadden, Skenderis, 2021)

Bzowski, McFadden, and Skenderis prove that the general scalar $n$-point function of *any* conformal field theory in momentum space can be written as a Feynman integral over an oriented $(n-1)$-simplex,
$$
\langle \mathcal{O}_1(p_1) \cdots \mathcal{O}_n(p_n) \rangle \;\propto\; \prod_{i<j} \int \frac{d^d q_{ij}}{(2\pi)^d}\, \frac{\hat{f}(\hat u)}{q_{ij}^{2\alpha_{ij} + d}}\, \prod_k (2\pi)^d \delta(p_k + \textstyle\sum_l q_{lk}),
$$
where $\hat f$ is an arbitrary function of $n(n-3)/2$ momentum-space cross ratios. This *simplex integral* is itself a generalised Feynman integral whose conformal invariance is established by reduction to *mesh integrals* (the case $\hat f = 1$) using a Mellin-Barnes representation, and equivalently by exhibiting the action of the special conformal Ward identity as a total derivative.

The paper derives the simplex representation of $n$-point contact Witten diagrams in holographic CFTs and shows that the function $\hat f$ for the contact diagram exhibits a remarkable recursive structure in $n$, enabled by a star-mesh duality of electrical-circuit theory. The number of integrations is $n - 2$, an improvement (for $n > 4$) over the $n(n-3)/2$ integrations of the Mellin representation.

### 7.2 Shift operators and the simplex (Caloro, 2024)

Caloro's thesis builds directly on the simplex representation to develop a parametric and operator-theoretic perspective. Key contributions include:

- **Cayley-Menger parametrisation.** Using *inverse* Schwinger parameters and regarding them as resistances in an electrical network, the simplex integral is re-parametrised in terms of effective resistances $V_{kl}$ between simplex vertices. The Schwinger exponential becomes diagonal, the graph polynomials acquire a determinantal structure based on the Cayley-Menger matrix, and the number of scalar integrals is reduced.
- **New $d \to d + 2$ shift operators.** Inspired by the determinantal structure and a Cayley-type identity (a Bernstein-Sato operator), Caloro constructs new families of *weight-shifting operators* expressible as determinants that send dimension $d$ to $d + 2$ for general $n$-point correlators. These generalise the known 3-point operators of Bzowski-McFadden-Skenderis to arbitrary $n$. The action of the standard $d$-preserving operators of Karateev, Kravchuk, and Simmons-Duffin is also recovered from the parametric perspective.
- **Direct verification of conformal Ward identities** for the new representation, without recourse to recursive arguments.

The thesis contains an appendix specifically on the *GKZ representation of Feynman integrals* in the context of momentum-space CFT, showing how to upgrade the Lee-Pomeransky representation by promoting the coefficients of every term in $F + s U$ to independent variables. The relevance for the present review is that conformal correlators in momentum space are *themselves* generalised Feynman integrals, and their conformal-invariance and shift structure carry over to Feynman integrals of the same topology (e.g., triple-K integrals understood as triangle Feynman integrals). The Landau singularities of the simplex integral are also analysed, and the Bernstein-Sato story provides a route to weight-shifting operators related to (but distinct from) the GKZ shift relations.

### 7.3 Cosmological polytopes (Arkani-Hamed, Benincasa, Postnikov, 2017)

Arkani-Hamed, Benincasa, and Postnikov develop a *positive-geometry* approach to the wavefunction of the universe in toy models of conformally coupled scalars in FRW cosmologies. For each Feynman graph $G$, they associate a *cosmological polytope* $\mathcal{P}_G$ in $\mathbb{P}^{E + V - 1}$ whose vertices are read off from the graph: each edge $e = (v, v')$ contributes three vertices $x_v + x_{v'} - y_e$, $x_v + y_e - x_{v'}$, $x_{v'} + y_e - x_v$. The contribution of $G$ to the wavefunction is identified with the *canonical form* $\Omega(Y; \mathcal{P}_G)$, which has logarithmic singularities precisely on the facets of $\mathcal{P}_G$.

Various physical representations of the wavefunction correspond to different *triangulations* of $\mathcal{P}_G$ (or its dual): the time-integral / "bulk" representation, old-fashioned perturbation theory, and tree-level recursion relations all appear as triangulations, while contour-integral and push-forward representations of the canonical form give entirely new representations of the wavefunction. The polytope geometrises the universal singularity structure: total-energy poles, partial-energy poles, and the scattering-amplitude facet (where $\sum_v x_v = 0$) is itself a simplex whose canonical form reproduces flat-space scattering amplitudes.

The connection to GKZ is conceptual rather than operational. Cosmological polytopes are *not* the Newton polytopes of Lee-Pomeransky polynomials, and their canonical forms are not solutions of GKZ systems in the strict sense. Yet the underlying philosophy is the same: the singularity structure of a class of Feynman-like integrals is encoded in a convex polytope, whose combinatorial structure (vertices, facets, triangulations, dual polytopes, symmetries) translates directly into physical structure (poles, propagators, alternative representations, symmetries of correlators). Reading the GKZ programme and the cosmological-polytope programme side by side suggests a broader vision in which different polyhedral structures classify different sectors of perturbation theory.

### 7.4 Mirror symmetry and earlier physics applications

Klausen (2020) reminds the reader that GKZ systems entered physics already in the early 1990s through the work of Hosono, Klemm, Theisen, and Yau on mirror symmetry of Calabi-Yau manifolds, where GKZ systems control the period integrals of toric Calabi-Yau hypersurfaces and complete intersections. The recent Calabi-Yau geometry of equal-mass banana integrals at $\ell$ loops, recently studied via GKZ systems by Klemm, Nega, and Safari, shows that this mirror-symmetric heritage of GKZ is now feeding back into Feynman-integral theory. Weinzierl (2022) similarly discusses the family of banana graphs as the simplest examples where higher-dimensional Calabi-Yau varieties (elliptic curve at two loops, K3 surface at three loops) appear in Feynman integrals.

## 8. Computational Aspects, Open Problems, and Limitations

### 8.1 Software

The GKZ programme is computationally demanding but increasingly well-supported. The papers in the corpus cite or rely on:

- **Macaulay2** (Grayson-Stillman) with the *D-modules* package for toric ideals, initial ideals, and standard pairs (de la Cruz, 2019, including a worked appendix).
- **PALP** (Kreuzer-Skarke), and its successors, for computing normal forms and automorphism groups of lattice polytopes (Grinis-Kasprzyk; de la Cruz, 2024).
- **Magma** and **Sage** ports of these algorithms (Grinis-Kasprzyk).
- **FeynGKZ** (Ananthanarayan, Banik, Bera, Datta, 2023), a Mathematica package automating the GKZ approach to Feynman integrals.
- **Olsson.wl** (Ananthanarayan, Bera, Friot, Pathak) for linear transformations of multivariate hypergeometric functions.
- Numerical / sector-decomposition tools such as *FIESTA* and *SecDec* are used for cross-checks, and tropical-integration approaches (Borinsky; Borinsky-Munch-Tellander) provide complementary routes.

### 8.2 Limitations

Several limitations of the GKZ approach are explicitly acknowledged in the corpus:

- **Codimension-zero matrices.** When the lattice $L = \ker A$ is trivial, the canonical-series algorithm produces no series; one must deform the polynomial and take a careful limit (de la Cruz, 2019).
- **Non-unimodular triangulations.** Klausen (2020)'s closed-form Theorem 3.2 is stated for unimodular triangulations; while extensions to regular non-unimodular triangulations are possible, the meromorphic prefactors are no longer easily determined.
- **On-shell graphs without unimodular triangulations.** The full massive on-shell bubble is the simplest counter-example; one recovers it as a limit of the off-shell version (whose polytope does admit a unimodular triangulation) or by dilating the polytope.
- **Non-generic parameters.** The rank-volume equality is guaranteed for very generic $\beta$. At the integer values of $d$ relevant to physics the parameters are typically resonant, which makes the system reducible (Schulze and Walther 2012) without changing its rank; the rank itself can exceed the volume only when the toric ring is not Cohen-Macaulay (Matusevich, Miller and Walther 2005), which the hypotheses of Klausen's thesis (Theorem 3.4.2) exclude. Resonant parameters must be handled carefully, typically through transformation formulas of the ${}_2F_1$ type that split divergent and convergent parts (Klausen 2020 Section 4 illustrates this for the full massive sunset).
- **Practical scaling.** The number of $\Gamma$-series in a GKZ representation equals the polytope volume, which grows rapidly. For high-loop, high-leg, or fully massive integrals the series count and dimension can be prohibitive; in such cases, only the symmetry / transformation theory may be tractable.

### 8.3 Open directions

The corpus suggests several directions of active development:

- **Kinematic singularities and principal A-determinants.** Klausen's later work (2022, cited in de la Cruz 2024) develops the principal A-determinant as the proper polytope-theoretic object for Landau analyses, pursuing a programme also developed in different language by Arkani-Hamed-Hillman-Mizera and Gardi et al.
- **Calabi-Yau geometry from GKZ.** The work of Klemm, Nega, and Safari on the $\ell$-loop banana via GKZ systems and relative Calabi-Yau periods opens a route to higher-genus / higher-Calabi-Yau Feynman integrals through GKZ technology.
- **Macaulay matrix and intersection numbers.** Chestnov, Gasparotto, Mandal, Mastrolia, Matsubara-Heo, and Munch have developed a Macaulay-matrix approach to Feynman integrals based on linear relations and intersection numbers in the GKZ setting.
- **D-module techniques.** Henn, Pratt, Sattelberger, and Zoia recently revisited D-module methods for solving differential equations of Feynman integrals in the GKZ framework.
- **Beyond scalar / Euclidean integrals.** Most of the existing GKZ literature treats scalar Euclidean integrals in dimensional regularisation. Tensorial, Minkowski-region, and infrared-divergent integrals require additional work, much of which is still in progress.

## 9. Conclusion

The papers reviewed here converge on a unified picture of Feynman integrals as A-hypergeometric functions in the sense of Gelfand-Kapranov-Zelevinsky. The Lee-Pomeransky representation packages the entire Feynman graph into a single polynomial $G$, whose monomial-exponent matrix $A$ defines a GKZ system. De la Cruz (2019) and Klausen (2020) prove independently that the generalised Feynman integral is annihilated by this system, and provide explicit constructions of solutions: the canonical series of Saito-Sturmfels-Takayama (de la Cruz) and the closed-form $\Gamma$-series sums over regular unimodular triangulations of the Newton polytope (Klausen). The framework subsumes convergence, meromorphic continuation, the count of master integrals, and the $\epsilon$-expansion, all controlled by the polytope geometry.

The transformation theory of Forsgård-Matusevich-Sobieska (2019), specialised to Feynman integrals by de la Cruz (2024), shows that classical hypergeometric transformation formulas are consequences of the symmetries of a single polytope, each an identity between the integral at two parameter vectors with the coefficients permuted. The IBP-like relations between Feynman integrals come from elsewhere, from the contiguity and toric operators of the GKZ system that earlier work on differential reduction exploits. The algorithmic infrastructure for working with such polytopes is provided by Grinis-Kasprzyk (2013) and Liu-Cai (2025), whose results on normal forms, automorphism groups, and the unimodular isomorphism problem set both the practical and theoretical limits of the approach.

Reading the GKZ literature alongside the simplex-integral programme of Bzowski-McFadden-Skenderis (2021) and Caloro (2024), and the cosmological-polytope programme of Arkani-Hamed-Benincasa-Postnikov (2017), suggests that the polyhedral perspective on perturbative quantum field theory extends well beyond GKZ systems narrowly construed. Conformal correlators, holographic Witten diagrams, cosmological wavefunctions, and ordinary Feynman integrals all admit descriptions in which a convex (or sometimes positive) geometry encodes the analytic, singular, and symmetric structure of physically meaningful integrals. Whether these threads, A-hypergeometric Feynman integrals, simplex correlators, cosmological polytopes, and the Calabi-Yau integrals of mirror-symmetric provenance, admit a deeper common framework is one of the outstanding questions in the field.

What is already settled is that the GKZ approach has changed the way we think about Feynman integrals. The classification of Feynman integrals is now, to a substantial degree, a classification of lattice polytopes, and the questions one asks of a graph, its singularities, master-integral count, possible series representations, transformation identities, are increasingly being answered by combinatorial and computational methods drawn from the geometry of polytopes and the theory of holonomic D-modules.
